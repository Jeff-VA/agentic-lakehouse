Logistic regression or XGBoost, and on which features?

The news classifier scores every burst the detector flags, so the dashboard can tell a story breaking from an edit war without asking Claude about each one. This study tests which model and which features do that best. Every score is out-of-sample, and no story ever lands on both sides of a train/test split.

bursts flagged labeled news, edit war or routine news, on articles

The short answer

  1. Logistic regression, not XGBoost, for now. LR on the v2 features scores average precision against tuned XGBoost's . Given the choice, tuning picked depth-1 trees for XGBoost, an additive model with no interactions, which is nearly what logistic regression already is.
  2. Features are the real gain. Choosing features honestly inside each training fold lifts average precision from (the deployed features) to , ahead in of resamples. Two features carry it: whether the wiki is large, and how busy its baseline is.
  3. The threshold is the bug users see. The live model's probabilities never reach 0.5, so it never calls news. Calling news at gives 50% precision and catches of news.
  4. The intervals are wide ( to for the best model), because the news labels cover only stories. The random-sample labels that started today are what will narrow them.

What the model learns from

Each row is a burst: an article with at least 6 edits by 3 editors in 30 minutes, at least 4σ above what's usual for an article on that wiki at that hour. Claude Haiku labels a sample of them as news, edit war, routine or unclear. The model predicts news (1) against edit war or routine (0), leaving out the unclear ones. News is of the labeled bursts.

Three facts about these labels shape every choice below.

  • Few positives. news labels come from only articles, because a growing story is flagged several times. The real sample size is closer to the number of stories than the number of rows, so every comparison here splits by article and reports an interval.
  • A skewed sample. Until today Claude read the first 10 bursts of each hour, so busy hours were the least covered (chart below). The model learned from, and was judged on, a sample tilted toward quiet hours.
  • A teacher who sees more. Claude reads the title, the edit summaries and what it knows of the world. The model sees a handful of numbers. Features that carry some of what Claude sees (a year in the title, how busy the article already was) are where the gains should come from.

Why logistic regression, and why Newton's method

Newton's method is how the model is fitted, not a modeling choice. Logistic regression with an L2 penalty minimizes one smooth, convex loss, so it has exactly one best set of weights, and any decent solver finds it. Newton's method uses the loss's curvature (a 7×7 matrix here) to reach it in a few steps. scikit-learn's default solver approximates that curvature instead. Fitted on the same data with the same penalty, the deployed Newton code and scikit-learn agree to within on every weight, a gap left by scikit-learn stopping at its default tolerance. Writing it in numpy kept scikit-learn out of the stack's image.

Logistic regression against XGBoost is the real question. XGBoost is usually the strongest choice for tabular data: it adds up hundreds of small trees, each correcting the last, and finds interactions nobody wrote down. That strength needs data. With about 30 news labels in a training fold, a tree can carve out one story per branch, and its probabilities tend to be overconfident on so few positives. A logistic regression has one weight per feature, can't memorize a story, and its log-loss fit keeps probabilities near the base rate unless the evidence is strong. It also deploys as a dozen numbers in an Iceberg table that the detector applies with arithmetic, and each weight reads as an effect in the paper. Those are reasons to expect LR to hold up here, not proof, so the rest of this page measures it, tuning both models the same way.

Eight models, scored the same way

Three feature sets and two model families:

  • Deployed: the six features the live model uses.
  • All : those six plus candidates from the burst's window, the article's recent past, the wiki's baseline and its size (listed under Which features carry it).
  • v2: the deployed six plus large wiki and year in title, the two candidates that each raised average precision when added alone, with surprise swapped for the usual edits it is measured against. Surprise moves with the edit count (correlation 0.93), so the two weights blow up in opposite directions; edits and the baseline are nearly uncorrelated (0.04), score the same, and give weights that read as effects.

v2 was picked by looking at these same labels, so its score flatters it. The honest check is LR, features picked in each fold: it reruns the picking (forward selection from the deployed six) inside every training fold, so the held-out fold never influences which features are used. XGBoost has its hyperparameters tuned over a 24-point grid inside every training fold, and logistic regression has its regularization tuned the same way. Would XGBoost win with more labels?

A fair worry is that XGBoost only needs more data. The learning curve trains each model on a quarter, half, three quarters and all of each training fold's articles, then tests on the same held-out folds.

Which features carry it

Medians by Claude's label (shares and yes/no features as averages). Bytes removed are printed the way a Wikipedia page history prints them. A feature can separate the labels well here and still add nothing to the model, when another feature already carries the same information.

What holds up. Wiki size and the baseline are the stable signals: forward selection picked large wiki in of training folds and usual edits in , and leaving either out of v2 costs about 0.07 to 0.12. Reverts matter as much, pushing the other way. Tuned XGBoost, given all the features, leans on the baseline most too.

What doesn't. Year in title looked strong alone (half of news titles carry a year) but adds little once wiki size is in: picked in of folds. Bot and anonymous shares slightly hurt v2; dropping them is a candidate once the random-sample labels can confirm it. The 20-language revert pattern measures reverts more correctly but predicts no better than the English one here.

A caution for the paper. Large wiki is the strongest feature, and of news labels are on large wikis. Part of that is real (big wikis cover breaking news fast), and part may be Claude recognizing English and French events more readily than others. The model would then learn Claude's coverage, not the world's. Per-tier precision on the random sample will show which.

Are the probabilities honest?

A calibrated model's 20% means news about one time in five. Points on the diagonal are calibrated; below it, the model is overconfident.

Where to draw the line

The live model calls news at 0.5, which it never reaches. A threshold should come from the precision the dashboard needs. These are chosen on v2 logistic regression's out-of-fold probabilities, and "news calls a day" applies each to every burst flagged so far, read or not.

50% precision means half the bursts called news are news, which suits a feed people scan. A threshold chosen on labels moves as labels arrive, so the step should recompute it every time it retrains and store it next to the weights.

Decisions

All six shipped on October 18, 2026. The live classifier now trains on the v2 features, calls news from the threshold it learns, and records each call on the burst (called_news).

1

Keep logistic regression Shipped

Tuned XGBoost scores on v2 and on every feature, against logistic regression's , and tuning chose C = , the deployed value. Revisit XGBoost by rerunning this study as random-sample labels accumulate, and switch when its learning curve crosses LR's.

2

Ship the v2 features Shipped

Large wiki and usual edits in place of surprise carry the gain; year in title is cheap and marginal. None needs a new data feed: the size tier is the wikis lookup the stack already maintains, the year is a regex on the title, and usual edits is already on every burst row. classifier.py's feature list and the detector change together, so training and serving compute the same features.

3

Learn the threshold with the weights Shipped

Call news at the probability that keeps precision at 50% or more: here, catching of news, about calls a day. The step stores it in gold.news_classifier, the detector records each burst's call, and the called_news() macro reads that call in place of a hard-coded 0.5.

4

Gate the model on what matters Shipped

The current gate (training accuracy above always answering "not news") passes almost any model: the live one cleared it by 0.6 points. The gate is now grouped out-of-fold average precision closing at least a fifth of the gap between random guessing and perfect, computed in the step with the same numpy fit.

5

Claude reads every fresh burst Shipped

The model is short of labels, not compute. Reading every fresh burst costs about $0.22 a day and doubles the labels; a burst that arrives with the budget spent records a chance of 0, so the sample stays one whose bias is known.
